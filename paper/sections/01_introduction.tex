\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Drug shortages arise from several interacting conditions, including manufacturing and quality failures, production delays, constrained raw materials, discontinuations, demand shocks, and regulatory delays. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) identifies manufacturing quality problems as the most common reported cause and notes that small supplier bases, limited production capacity, and long lead times make older sterile injectables particularly vulnerable \cite{fda2019shortages,fdaShortageFAQ}. The World Health Organization has likewise linked medicine shortages to products with few manufacturers, difficult formulations, and tightly constrained shelf lives \cite{who2016shortages}. A shortage record can identify an affected presentation, company, availability state, and reported reason. It does not, by itself, explain which quality-management, supplier-control, change-management, or risk-communication principles an analyst should consult.

Those principles appear in long regulatory documents. ICH quality guidelines cover stability, analytical validation, impurities, good manufacturing practice, pharmaceutical development, quality risk management, quality systems, and lifecycle management \cite{ichQualityGuidelines}. FDA guidance relates current good manufacturing practice to modern quality systems and risk management \cite{fdaQualitySystems2006}. European GMP Annex 11 addresses computerized systems, validation, data integrity, and continuity \cite{ecAnnex11}. Analysts therefore need to recover two different forms of evidence: citable source passages from regulatory documents and structured facts that connect shortages to products, active ingredients, and manufacturers.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) gives language models access to external evidence \cite{lewis2020rag}. Pharmaceutical regulatory retrieval presents a narrower and more auditable problem than open-domain question answering. Regulatory documents contain nested headings, short clauses whose scope depends on parent sections, and tables whose conditions can be fragmented during PDF conversion. Exact identifiers and specialist terms often matter. Structured shortage records add another representation problem: an NDC, manufacturer, active ingredient, and shortage reason are relations among entities, not interchangeable text passages.

Prior regulatory NLP studies show both the value and the limits of semantic retrieval. BERT-based section classification can organize unstructured drug-label text \cite{gray2023regulatory}. Pharmaceutical question-entailment models can retrieve related regulatory questions, but require domain-specific annotated data \cite{saraswat2023regulatory}. Recent RAG prototypes have searched FDA guidance or laboratory regulations \cite{proestel2025semantic,nanua2025raven}. More general regulatory information retrieval research has also shown that semantic similarity can conflict with legally meaningful relations and that neural rerankers may inherit inconsistent supervision \cite{chalkidis2021regir}. These findings motivate an evaluation centered on source passage recovery rather than fluent answer generation.

We make four contributions. First, we define regulatory passage retrieval and structured supply-chain fact verification as separate evaluation tasks. A graph node cannot substitute for a citable passage, and a semantically similar passage cannot substitute for a recorded product relation. Second, we build a provenance-preserving corpus and heterogeneous evidence graph that retain document, section, source chunk, table, summary, citation, shortage-event, NDC, ingredient, and manufacturer relations. Third, we compare direct chunk retrieval, hierarchical document routing, bounded graph traversal, lexical and dense retrieval, generated index-side profiles, fusion, and selective reranking under fixed evaluation protocols. Fourth, we construct a method-blinded 60-query candidate pool, obtain independent labels from two reviewers, retain the original labels, adjudicate all disagreements, and execute a one-shot 58-query formal run after excluding two questions with incomplete evidence.

The study does not predict how long a shortage will last or estimate patient harm. It evaluates whether a system can locate supporting regulatory text and expose a checkable path through public shortage facts. This boundary keeps every reported result tied to a frozen source, an explicit relevance judgment, or a provenance-bearing graph edge.
